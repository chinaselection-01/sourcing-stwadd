Private label is your brand on their factory

Private label is often described as simply putting your logo on a product. In practice it is a set of decisions about manufacturing, design rights, packaging and supply that determine whether your product stays yours.

OEM and ODM are different contracts

OEM means you buy a product a factory already makes and apply your brand. You specify capacity, material, colour and decoration, and the factory executes. It is faster, cheaper and lower risk, and it is the right starting point unless appearance is your entire competitive advantage.

ODM means the factory designs the product and you sell it under your brand. You are buying a design service plus a production run. It gives you differentiation, but you need to establish clearly who owns the design.

The distinction matters because a design you do not own is a product your supplier can sell to anyone, including your direct competitor, and it is entirely legal unless you have an agreement in place.

The clauses that protect your product

Design ownership and exclusivity. State in writing who owns the design after delivery, whether the factory is prohibited from selling that design to others in your market, and for how long. An NDA alone does not create exclusivity and does not stop the factory from selling the same product; it only creates a remedy after the fact. If exclusivity matters, say so and get it signed before tooling starts.

Tooling ownership. If you fund the mould, it should be your property, marked with your name, listed in an annex, and you should be able to remove it if you move production. Without that written clause the factory can retain it and force you to re-order at their price.

Packaging ownership. Artwork, colour boxes and printed materials made to your specification are yours. State this, because print assets are easy for a factory to reuse.

Minimum pricing and non-circumvention. Your agreed price should be the price for your volume band, and your customers should not be approached directly. Both are enforceable if written down and both are absent from most informal arrangements.

Protecting your artwork and specifications

Send files through a channel that records delivery, and keep a signed copy of the specification, artwork approval and approved sample. In a dispute, the question is rarely who is right but who can produce documentation.

Register your trademark in the markets you sell into before you launch. A private-label programme built on an unregistered brand can be copied legally, and the cost of registration is trivial next to the cost of defending a brand you cannot protect.

Consider marking. A discreet mould mark or internal part number on the product itself identifies who made it in a way a packaging change cannot erase, which is useful if you ever need to investigate a diverted shipment.

When private label pays and when it does not

It pays when the product is undifferentiated and your advantage is distribution, pricing or an existing customer relationship. In that case OEM on a stock mould is efficient, fast and low-risk.

It does not pay when your entire proposition is the product itself, because a factory that can make it for you can make it for your competitor. That is the moment the mould, the design or a real technical advantage has to belong to you.

The most common private-label failure is not a copy of your product. It is a range that is undifferentiated at the shelf and slightly more expensive than the next item over, with no reason for a shopper to choose you.

What to check before you approve the first sample

Approve the physical sample, not the photograph, and record what you approved. Test the function rather than the finish: does it seal, does it leak, does it hold temperature, does the closure survive repeated use. Check the barcode scans, and check that the packaging print matches your artwork exactly, because a colour shift that looks acceptable on screen is visible on a shelf.

Keep the approved sample. It is the reference for the pre-production check and the reference if a dispute arises six months later.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between OEM and ODM?

OEM means the factory makes a product to your specification and you apply your branding. ODM means the factory designs the product and you sell it under your brand. OEM is faster and lower risk; ODM gives differentiation but requires clear agreements about who owns the resulting design.

Can my supplier sell the same private label product to my competitors?

Unless you have an exclusivity clause in writing, yes, and it is entirely legal. A non-disclosure agreement alone does not prevent it. If exclusivity in your market matters, state it and get it signed before tooling or production begins.

Who owns the mould if I pay for it?

You should, provided the contract says so explicitly: the mould is paid for by the buyer, is buyer property in the factory, is marked with the buyer's name, and may be removed if the buyer moves production. Without that written clause the factory can retain it.

Do I need a trademark before starting private label?

Register it in the markets you sell into before launch. A private-label programme built on an unregistered brand can be legally copied, and trademark registration costs very little compared with defending a brand you are unable to protect.
