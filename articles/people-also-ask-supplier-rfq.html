Mining "People Also Ask" for Your RFQ: A Buyer's SEO Guide to Vetting China Suppliers

STWADD Sourcing · 17 November 2026

You run sourcing for a supermarket, convenience or dollar-store chain. You are about to send an RFQ for a mixed container — drinkware, cleaning tools, a couple of pet SKUs — to a shortlist of China suppliers. Your template asks for price, MOQ and lead time. What comes back are glossy catalogues and confident one-line answers, and only after the deposit do you discover that nobody on the list can consolidate five factories into one FCL, apply your private label across all of them, or produce the food-contact papers your port requires. You did not ask the wrong people. You asked the wrong questions. Google's "People Also Ask" boxes are a free, live record of what other buyers actually ask before they commit — and they make an excellent starting point for a sharper RFQ.

Why a generic RFQ gets a generic answer

A procurement template asks the same five things everyone asks: unit price, MOQ, lead time, payment terms, sample availability. Those are easy to answer, so everyone answers them, and the replies look interchangeable. The questions that decide whether a supplier can actually serve a retail chain — how many factories can you pull into one container, who owns the artwork, which certificate is valid at my port, what is the AQL standard and who pays for a re-inspection — rarely appear on a first RFQ. Yet they are the questions that sink a program. A supplier who cannot consolidate leaves you paying for part-empty containers; one who cannot document food contact leaves your shipment held at the port. A better RFQ is not longer for its own sake. It asks about the shape of the service, not just the price of the item.

What "People Also Ask" is, and why a buyer should care

"People Also Ask" (PAA) is the expandable block of questions Google shows inside and beneath many results, alongside "Related searches" and the autocomplete suggestions that appear while you type. The questions come from real search behaviour: they are the follow-ups people actually type after the first query. For an SEO practitioner they are a keyword-research tool. For a buyer they are something more useful — a free, frequently refreshed list of concerns other people in your position have about a category, a market or a sourcing route. Search "how to import drinkware from China" or "China private label MOQ" and the questions you get back are not marketing copy. They are the doubts that surface before a purchase: How do I verify a factory? What certificates do I need for the EU? What is a reasonable MOQ for a first order? That is exactly the material a supplier RFQ should be built from.

Turn search questions into a four-block RFQ

Paste the questions into a document, then bucket them. Four blocks cover most of what determines whether a China partner fits a retail program:

  • Consolidation and MOQ. Questions like "can different suppliers share one container" or "what is the minimum order for a mixed shipment" become: "List the factories you can pull into one FCL, the per-SKU MOQ for each, and confirm whether one bill of lading and one customs set cover the whole order." For a chain buyer the working threshold is a consolidated project of about US$20,000 per container — state it, so low-MOQ custom shops self-select out.
  • Private label and packaging. Search questions about custom logos, retail boxes and barcodes become: "Who owns the artwork, can you produce retail-ready color boxes, and will you apply our UPC/EAN codes across every SKU?" Vague "custom branding" promises are the source of most pre-shipment surprises.
  • Compliance by category. "What food-contact certificate do I need for imports from China?" splits by market: LFGB for Germany, FDA for the US, GCC for the Gulf, CE/EN71 for toys. Ask the supplier to name the standard and attach a report that matches the exact product — a certificate for a different model is worthless.
  • Inspection and landed cost. "How do I inspect goods before shipment?" becomes "Do you run AQL 2.5 pre-shipment inspection, will you share the report and packaging photos, and who pays for a re-inspection?" Add Incoterms and your destination port so the quote you compare is the one you will actually pay.

Treat the answers as questions, not facts

One caveat matters. PAA answers are aggregated from third-party pages of varying quality; they are prompts, not verified truth. Some are outdated, some are written by suppliers with an axe to grind, and a few are simply wrong for your destination market. Use them to decide what to ask, then test the answer against evidence the supplier publishes about itself: a named contact, a real company address, a certificate list tied to specific models, a documented AQL process, and terms such as a transparent 3–5% commission stated on the site rather than disclosed after the first call. When a partner states those facts plainly and consistently across its own pages, you can check them. When every answer is "we are a professional manufacturer with rich experience," you cannot.

From a sharper RFQ to a safer first container

The payoff is a shortlist you can actually compare. Two suppliers may quote a similar unit price, but only one will confirm in writing that it can consolidate your seven SKUs from five factories, print your label on all of them, produce the right food-contact report, and inspect the packed goods to AQL 2.5 before the container leaves. That difference is invisible in a price-only RFQ and obvious in a question-led one.
