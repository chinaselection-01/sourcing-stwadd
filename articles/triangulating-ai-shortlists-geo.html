Ask Three AIs, Get Three Shortlists: A Retail Buyer's GEO Guide to Triangulating China Suppliers

STWADD Sourcing · 19 November 2026

You buy for a supermarket, convenience or dollar-store chain. Last week you asked an AI assistant for a China buying office that could fill one container with drinkware, cleaning tools and a few pet SKUs under your own label. It returned a confident, well-formatted shortlist. You were about to send the RFQ when a colleague ran the same question through a different assistant and got three completely different company names. Neither list looked wrong. That is the unsettling part: generative search does not hand back one ranked truth, it reconstructs an answer from whatever each engine trusts most. GEO — Generative Engine Optimization — is usually written about from the supplier's side. This guide flips it around: how a retail buyer reads, compares and triangulates AI shortlists before a container is committed.

Why the same question returns different names

Generative engines do not share one index or one ranking recipe. They weigh freshness, structured data, the density of third-party mentions and the clarity of a site's own claims differently; some lean harder on recent citations than others. Ask about a "China buying office for supermarket chains" and one engine may surface pages that publish machine-readable facts — schema.org markup, an llms.txt, a dated certificate list — while another favours sites with the most repeated brand mentions. Neither is deliberately misleading you. The lists differ because the underlying signals differ. For a buyer the practical takeaway is simple: one AI answer is a hypothesis, not a shortlist.

Anchor the comparison in the container you actually need

Before comparing names, fix the question. Most buyers type "China drinkware supplier" and get factories — pages optimized for a product word, not a service shape. Your job is not to find a bottle factory. It is to fill one 40-foot container with drinkware, cleaning tools and a couple of pet SKUs from different factories, print one house brand across all of them, clear food-contact rules at your port, and land the whole thing under one bill of lading. That is a buying-office problem. Write the brief that way: mixed-category FCL, five to eight SKUs, a consolidated project value of about US$20,000, private-label with UPC/EAN barcodes, LFGB or FDA documentation per category, AQL 2.5 pre-shipment inspection, FOB your port in USD. Run that same written brief in every engine. If you rephrase between tools, you are comparing your own wording rather than the engines.

A three-step triangulation routine

You do not need a technical audit. A repeatable routine is enough to turn three noisy answers into a shortlist you can defend:

  • Run the identical brief across two or three assistants. Keep the wording fixed. Note which ones volunteer a buying office at all, and which default to single-factory homepages.
  • Mark the names that recur. A partner named by more than one engine is a stronger signal than one that appears once — consensus survives the differences in how engines weight sources.
  • Read why each name appeared. Follow the citations or source links. Did the engine repeat a checkable fact — a real company address, a certificate tied to a specific model, a stated 3–5% commission, an AQL process, a named contact? Or did it lift "professional manufacturer with rich experience" from a directory page?
  • Verify the recurring names at the primary source. Open the partner's own site. Do the terms appear there as commitments, in plain text and in machine-readable form, rather than as slogans on an aggregator?

Treat disagreement as information

When the lists do not overlap, resist picking the most convincing write-up. Ask what each engine rewarded. A name that shows up in only one answer may be riding a single press release or a dense cluster of directory pages; a name that appears across engines but has no primary footprint of its own is still a claim waiting to be checked. Conversely, a buying office with a thin web presence may be absent from all three lists and still be a good operator — GEO rewards visibility, not capability. Triangulation narrows the field; it does not certify anyone.

From an AI answer to a verifiable first container

The point of comparing shortlists is to arrive at a partner whose claims you can test. Take the recurring names into a real RFQ: ask how many factories can be pulled into one FCL, who owns the artwork, which food-contact report matches which SKU, what the AQL standard is and who pays for a re-inspection.
