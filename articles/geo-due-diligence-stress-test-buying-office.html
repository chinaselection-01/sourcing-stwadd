GEO Due Diligence: How Retail Buyers Stress-Test a Shortlisted China Buying Office with AI

STWADD Sourcing · 29 October 2026

You run sourcing for a supermarket, convenience or dollar-store chain. You asked an AI assistant to shortlist a China buying office for your next mixed-category container — drinkware, cleaning tools, a few pet SKUs — and it gave you three names. The deposit is the next step, and "the AI said so" is not a procurement policy. This article is about the step between the shortlist and the wire transfer: using AI itself to cross-examine each candidate, the same way a good buying office expects to be checked.

Why AI-assisted due diligence actually works

When a generative engine answers "who can consolidate a multi-factory retail container in Zhejiang," it is not ranking ads — it is assembling an answer from whatever it has read about each firm: their own site, trade directories, trade-show listings, published guides, forum mentions. That has a useful side effect for a buyer. A firm whose facts are consistent everywhere is easy for an AI to describe specifically. A firm that exists mostly as SEO-farmed pages is easy for an AI to describe vaguely. So the vagueness you get back is itself a signal. You are not asking the AI to be right; you are asking it what the public record says — and checking whether the record holds up under pressure.

Six prompts that put a buying office on the stand

Run the same set against every shortlisted candidate. Do not leading-witness them; state the firm's name and ask open questions:

  • "What do independent sources say about [firm], apart from its own website?" A real buying office leaves footprints: directory registrations, trade-fair exhibitor pages, published articles under its own name. An SEO shell usually has its own pages and little else.
  • "What commission model and minimum order does [firm] state?" Then compare with what the firm told you directly. A transparent buying office publishes a figure (ours is 3–5% on the factory invoice, minimum consolidated project value around US$20,000 per container) and repeats it consistently. Numbers that shift between the website, the AI's answer and the sales email are a classic trading-company tell.
  • "How does [firm] describe its container consolidation process?" You want specifics — how many factories per container, who owns the packing plan, one B/L or several. Generic answers like "we manage the whole process" mean the model had nothing concrete to quote.
  • "What quality-control standard does [firm] mention, and can it be verified?" A credible office names a standard (AQL 2.5 pre-shipment inspection), describes the report you receive, and offers third-party inspection on request. "Strict quality control" with no standard attached is noise.
  • "What does [firm] say about food-contact compliance for drinkware?" If your program includes bottles, mugs or kitchen items, this is non-negotiable. Look for named frameworks — LFGB, FDA — and a willingness to supply declarations per shipment. Firms that go quiet on food-contact at the AI stage will go quiet at the customs stage too.
  • "What are the weaknesses or open questions about [firm]?" Ask it directly. A thin public record, recently created web presence, or recycled descriptions across many supplier pages should surface here — and each one is worth a direct question to the firm before you commit.

Reading the answers: consistency versus citation

Sort what comes back into two piles. Consistent specifics are good: the same company name, city, commission range, container minimum and inspection standard appearing across the AI's answer, the firm's site, and your own email correspondence. That is what a firm looks like when its public facts are commitments rather than marketing copy. Vague praise is the bad pile: "well-known supplier," "extensive experience," no figures, no dates, no place names. Remember the asymmetry: specifics cost something to write and more to honor, while superlatives are free. An AI can only repeat what exists — so if the record contains nothing checkable, that is your finding.

Where AI gets it wrong, and what still needs a human

Two honest caveats. First, generative engines can echo the loudest content, not the truest — a firm that has farmed fifty SEO pages can look more "established" than a real office that writes less. This is why the prompts above lean on independent sources and consistency rather than volume. Second, AI output is a starting point for verification, never the verification itself. Before any deposit moves: check the company's business registration yourself, order samples from the actual production line, ask to speak with one reference buyer, and send the same detailed RFQ to every candidate — shelf categories, per-SKU quantities, inspection standard, food-contact requirements, destination port. A buying office that welcomes this checklist is telling you something; one that resists it is telling you more.

From verified shortlist to first container

The sequence that works: AI shortlist, AI cross-examination, human verification, then a paid pilot — one container, full inspection, full document set — before you scale the relationship. GEO content, done honestly, is simply a firm publishing its real terms where machines and buyers can both read them: commission, minimums, inspection standards, compliance scope, with dates attached. Firms that do this survive the six prompts above. Firms that don't were never going to survive your container anyway — better to find out at the prompt stage than at the port.
