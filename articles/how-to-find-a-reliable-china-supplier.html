How to find a reliable China supplier without learning it the expensive way

Most supplier problems are discovered after the container leaves, when there is no leverage and the goods cannot be sold. The checks below are the ones that catch problems before that happens, ordered by how much risk each one removes.

Start with the registry, not the website

Every serious China supplier has a website, and every trading company has a well-designed one. A website proves nothing. The registry proves the entity exists.

Search the company name in China's National Enterprise Credit Information Publicity System at gsxt.gov.cn. You will find registration status, registered capital, the legal representative, the business scope, and any penalties or abnormal-operations listings. A company under an abnormal-operations status has failed to submit filings or is being pursued, which is a reason to look elsewhere.

Three things to check specifically. The business scope should cover your product category, because a trading company registered to sell electronics may not be permitted to manufacture what you asked for. The registered capital should be plausible relative to the scale of the orders you intend to place. And the legal representative should match the person who signs your contract.

Then check the entity on your contract matches the entity on the payment instruction and the invoice. Mismatches between these three documents are a well-worn fraud pattern, and they are the single most useful thing to verify before you send money.

Understand which counterparty you are actually dealing with

There are three legitimate arrangements, and the difference is mostly about who buys, who manufactures and who is accountable.

Factory direct. You contract with the factory that makes the goods. Lowest unit price because there is one margin in the chain. You also own supplier verification, production follow-up and quality control, which is work that requires someone who can read a Chinese quote and stand in a factory.

Sourcing agent or buying office. You instruct someone to buy on your behalf. They shortlist factories, run RFQs, coordinate samples, inspect and consolidate. You pay a disclosed fee, usually 3 to 8 percent against the factory invoice, and you keep visibility of the factory invoice.

Trading company. One counterparty buys from the factory and resells to you. Simplest administration, one contract, one contact. The factory invoice stays private and the price contains a margin you cannot see, typically 15 to 30 percent.

None of these is a scam. Trading companies exist because small buyers genuinely need them. The mistake is not choosing one, it is choosing one without knowing which one you chose, and then comparing its price against a factory quote as if they were the same product.

The four questions that expose a trading company

Ask these during a video call, casually, and listen to whether the answers are specific or evasive.

Can I watch this step running on the production line? A factory agrees immediately and shows you. A trading company offers a showroom tour and changes the subject.

Where is the tooling stored, and is it marked with my name? A factory points at a rack and tells you which mould is yours. A trading company cannot answer because there is no rack.

Can my third-party inspector attend before the container is sealed? A factory with confidence in its work says yes without blinking. Objection usually means the goods are not ready, production was subcontracted somewhere you have not seen, or the order is not what was quoted.

Who pays if the goods do not match the approved sample? A factory answers according to your contract clause. A trading company gives a vague assurance because it does not yet have that conversation with the factory.

Verify the documents actually match the product

Test reports are the most commonly misused document in China sourcing. A report is valid for the specific model, material and colourway it was issued for. It does not cover a different batch, a different colour or a different size, and a generic "food grade certificate" without a test report means nothing.

When you receive a report, check four things: the model number on the report matches the model you are ordering, the date is recent rather than inherited from an older project, the test items cover what your market requires rather than only what the factory happened to test, and the laboratory is accredited. A material certificate for 304 steel is worth asking for separately, because substituting 201 looks identical and costs less.

The same principle applies to your contract. Attach the specification, the approved sample reference, the artwork and the defect classification as annexes. In a dispute nine months later, the question is rarely who is right but who can produce the document.

Structure the first order so it cannot hurt you much

The purpose of a first order is not margin. It is information: learning how this supplier behaves under a real order.

Keep it small and singular. One product, one factory, a quantity you could sell or write off without damaging your cash position. A US$3,000 test order that goes wrong costs you a weekend. A US$60,000 order that goes wrong costs you a season.

Test function, not finish. Samples get judged on whether they leak, whether they hold temperature and whether the closure survives repeated use. Appearance is the easiest thing to photograph and the hardest thing to judge, so do not let a beautiful sample carry the decision.

Inspect before loading, not after shipping. Once the container is sealed, your leverage is gone. A pre-shipment inspection costs a few hundred dollars and is the difference between reworking a lot at a factory and replacing a container you have already paid to ship.

Protect the payment. An irrevocable letter of credit, or a deposit with the balance against a copy of the bill of lading. Avoid full prepayment to an account that does not match the contract entity, and avoid payment methods with no recourse.

Frequently asked questions

How do I check whether a China supplier is a real company?

Search the company name in China's National Enterprise Credit Information Publicity System at gsxt.gov.cn. It shows registration status, registered capital, legal representative and any penalties. Then confirm the entity on your contract matches the entity on the invoice and the payment instruction.

What is the difference between a factory and a trading company?

A factory manufactures your product and you contract with the maker. A trading company buys from a factory and resells to you, keeping the factory invoice private and earning a margin inside the unit price, typically 15 to 30 percent. Both are legitimate; the mistake is not knowing which one you chose.

How do I verify a factory is really producing my product?

Ask for a live video walkthrough of the production floor rather than a showroom, ask which machine runs each step, ask where tooling is stored and whether it carries your name, and require a pre-production sample made on the actual production line before the bulk run.

What should a first order look like to limit risk?

One product and a small quantity, a tested sample, a pre-shipment inspection before sealing, and payment protection such as an irrevocable letter of credit or a deposit with the balance against a copy of the bill of lading.
