From a messy procurement spreadsheet to a priced supplier shortlist

Most buying lists do not arrive as clean Excel files. They arrive as a photo, a PDF, or a half-finished sheet with three languages in one column. Here is the workflow that turns that into a priced, verified shortlist — and what each step actually requires.

Start from whatever you have

The input does not need to be tidy. A clean Excel or CSV is ideal, but a phone photo of a supplier's catalogue page, a scanned PDF, or a list of items with images all work, because the first step is to structure the input, not to judge its formatting. What each line needs is enough to identify the product: a description, an image, or a target spec. If a line has at least one of those, it can be processed.

This is the part AI does best and people hate most — re-keying a messy table into something comparable. Let the model do it.

Step 1 — Structure the list

AI reads the input and produces a consistent table: item, description, spec, quantity, target price (if given), and a notes column. Mixed languages get translated, obvious duplicates get flagged, and impossible values (a 5-gram "bottle") get surfaced rather than silently carried forward.

Output: one clean sheet, every line comparable to the next.

Step 2 — Match each line to suppliers

For every row, AI matches the item to candidate suppliers and pulls an indicative price range from public data. This is the budget anchor: before any negotiation, you can see which lines are cheap, which are expensive, and which quote is an outlier.

Important: at this stage the price is an estimate. It is the starting point for verification, not the answer.

Step 3 — A human verifies on 1688

This is the step AI cannot do. A person on our team opens each supplier's 1688 store, confirms it is a real, active store with stock, checks that the product matches the spec, and records the lowest workable unit price. The AI found the candidate; the human confirms the reality. The price that comes back to your sheet is one a person stood behind.

Step 4 — Fill the table back

The verified prices, the supplier notes and a consolidation suggestion are written straight back into your spreadsheet and returned to you. You now have a priced shortlist, not a pile of screenshots.

From here the normal buying-office process takes over: samples, factory audit, AQL 2.5 inspection, private-label packaging, container consolidation and freight to your port.

What comes back is a shortlist, not a promise

Be clear about the boundary. The returned table carries an indicative, verified-lowest price per line for budgeting and comparison. A binding quote still requires a formal RFQ with confirmed quantity, material, decoration, carton and destination port. The spreadsheet gets you to a shortlist fast; the quote closes the exact numbers. That separation is what keeps the process honest.

Frequently asked questions

What format does my list need to be in?

Any format that carries the information. A clean Excel or CSV is ideal, but a photo of a table, a PDF, or a list with images all work — AI structures the input first. What matters is that each line has enough to identify the product: a description, an image, or a target spec.

Will the prices come back as a binding quote?

No. The returned table carries an indicative, verified-lowest price per line for budgeting and comparison. A binding quote still requires a formal RFQ with confirmed quantity, material, decoration, carton and destination port.

How is the price verified if AI cannot confirm a factory?

A person on our team opens each supplier's 1688 store, confirms it is real and stocked, and records the lowest workable unit price. AI does the matching; the human does the verification.
