Sourcing retail merchandise from China without over-ordering the wrong thing

The hard part of China sourcing for retail is not finding factories. It is deciding what to bring, in what quantity, and what it costs once it is on your shelf. Those three questions are where most retail programs either succeed quietly or quietly stop.

What sells from China and what does not

Retail programs sourced from China reliably succeed with products that are stable in demand, long in shelf life, easy to inspect and not fashion-driven. They reliably fail with products that are seasonal in an unpredictable way, sized for a specific market, or depend on a certification you cannot get.

Categories that work: drinkware and kitchen storage, home organisation, cleaning and household care, pet supplies, toys and kids basics, home textiles, garden and outdoor, hardware and small tools, seasonal and party items with a fixed date, and packaging and promotional goods.

Categories that need much more caution: anything electrical with certification requirements, anything sized to a specific body standard, anything with a seasonal demand curve you cannot forecast, and anything where a return rate above five percent makes the unit economics unworkable.

Realistic minimum order quantities

The category you choose sets your MOQ more than the factory does.

Low MOQ, 500 to 1,000 pieces: drinkware, kitchen items and small home goods on stock moulds with your logo. This is where most retail chains should start, because it tests the supplier relationship without a large cash commitment.

Medium MOQ, 2,000 to 5,000 pieces: custom-printed or private-label items where packaging and decoration are unique to you, and anything from a new factory you have not yet worked with.

High MOQ, 10,000 pieces and above: custom-moulded products, seasonal items, and anything where you have commissioned a specific colour or shape. Tooling runs from roughly US$1,500 to US$3,000 per mould, which is why factories protect their minimum here.

Two levers reduce MOQ more often than buyers expect: using stock moulds and accepting a mixed container where the total meets the factory's threshold rather than each item doing so separately.

Landed cost is not the unit price

A unit price from a factory is roughly a third of what the item costs you on the shelf. The rest is built from costs that are invisible until you list them.

Add: tooling amortised over your run, decoration and packaging, samples, third-party inspection, inland trucking from the factory to the port, export documentation, ocean freight, insurance, duty, customs brokerage, port handling, demurrage risk, and your inbound freight from the port to your distribution centre.

Two costs catch new importers by surprise. The first is demurrage, charged per day per container when a container sits beyond the free time at the destination port, which can exceed the entire ocean freight on a small program. The second is duty and the classification of your product, because vacuum and non-vacuum drinkware can fall under different tariff lines and the difference is material.

How to plan a buying calendar that arrives on time

Work backwards from the date the stock must be in your distribution centre, not from the order date.

Allow two to four weeks of ocean transit, three to ten days for customs clearance, two to five days for port handling and inbound delivery, and one to two weeks of buffer for the things that always happen. That gives you the date the container must be loaded. Subtract 20 to 35 days of production, then sample time of one to three weeks, then any tooling time of four to eight weeks.

For a Christmas or Black Friday season, place the production order three to four months ahead. Factories run near capacity before those seasons and their prices rise to fill the same limited slots, so a late order usually costs more and arrives later than planned.

What to fix in the contract before you order

Write these down: the specification with drawings and an approved sample reference, the quantity with an agreed tolerance, the delivery date with a notice period for schedule changes, the AQL level and defect classification, packaging and marking, tooling ownership, payment milestones, the period for raising a claim, and the dispute resolution route.

The two clauses retail buyers most often leave out are tooling ownership and the claim window. Tooling ownership protects you if you move the program, and a claim window stops a defect discovered six months after arrival becoming an unfalsifiable argument.

Frequently asked questions

What is a realistic MOQ for importing from China?

From around 500 pieces for drinkware and small goods on stock moulds with your own logo, rising to 2,000 to 5,000 for custom-printed or private-label items, and 10,000 or more for custom-moulded or seasonal products. Mixing several designs into one mixed container is the most common way to reduce the effective MOQ.

How do I calculate landed cost from a factory quote?

Start with the unit price and add tooling amortised over your run, decoration, packaging, samples, inspection, inland trucking, export documentation, ocean freight, insurance, duty, customs brokerage, port handling and inbound freight. A factory unit price is typically only about a third of the delivered cost.

How long does a China import order take end to end?

Realistically 10 to 16 weeks: one to three weeks for sampling and tooling, 20 to 35 days of production, two to three days for inspection, and 25 to 45 days of ocean transit depending on origin and destination, plus three to ten days for customs clearance.

Which product categories import best from China?

Stable-demand, long-shelf-life categories that are easy to inspect: drinkware, kitchen storage, home organisation, cleaning supplies, pet products, toys and kids basics, home textiles, hardware, seasonal items with a fixed date, and promotional goods. Electrical products and anything requiring a specific certification need considerably more work.
