Mining a Supplier's Own Site: Search Operators That Surface Real MOQ, Certificates and Factory Facts

STWADD Sourcing · 3 November 2026

You run a supermarket, convenience or dollar-store sourcing program. You have a shortlist of China buying offices or factories, but every homepage reads like a brochure — "top supplier," "strict quality control," "one-stop service." None of it tells you the three numbers that decide whether a partner fits your container: the real per-SKU MOQ, the actual food-contact certificate on file, and whether they can print UPC barcodes and retail-ready boxes. You can stop guessing from the homepage. Search engines let you query a supplier's own domain directly and pull the documents and spec pages their marketing front-end hides. Here is how a retail buyer uses search operators to mine a partner's real facts before spending a cent on an RFQ.

Why the homepage hides the facts you need

Marketing pages are written to rank and to reassure, not to answer a procurement review. The figures that decide a fit for a consolidating buyer — per-SKU MOQ, AQL acceptance level, LFGB/FDA documentation, factory registration, barcode and private-label capability — usually live deeper: in PDF certificates, spec sheets, certification pages, and the "about" or "compliance" sections that almost nobody links from the hero banner. A focused query against the supplier's own domain surfaces those pages directly, skipping the brochure and landing you on the evidence. For a buyer who needs to compare five candidates before a single container, that shortcut is worth more than any "trusted worldwide" slogan.

Five operators every importer should know

You do not need to be an SEO specialist to use these. They work in Google, Bing and most regional search engines. Replace the domain with your candidate's:

  • site: — restrict results to one domain. Try site:stwadd.com MOQ to see every page on that domain that mentions MOQ.
  • site: + "exact phrase" — force an exact claim. site:stwadd.com "AQL 2.5" finds the pages where they actually commit to an inspection standard, not just "strict QC."
  • filetype: — pull only documents. filetype:pdf site:stwadd.com LFGB digs the real certificate PDFs out of the site, the ones you can open and verify, instead of a "certified" badge.
  • inurl: — find pages by URL pattern. inurl:certification site:stwadd.com or inurl:compliance jumps straight to the compliance section a homepage never shows you.
  • "US$20,000" / specific numbers — search the exact figure a shortlist partner quoted you. If a buying office claims a ~US$20,000 container minimum on the phone but the number never appears anywhere on their own site, that gap is itself a signal.

The point of an operator is precision. A plain search for "China drinkware supplier" returns ten thousand results; site:stwadd.com "AQL 2.5" filetype:pdf returns the one document proving the inspection standard your program requires. Specificity is how you shrink a vendor's marketing surface down to the facts.

What to dig for before you send an RFQ

Run the same small set of queries against each shortlisted domain and collect the answers in a one-page comparison. The facts a consolidating retail buyer actually needs:

  • Compliance documents: LFGB, FDA, CE, EN71, RoHS — use filetype:pdf searches and open the originals. For food-contact drinkware and kitchenware, this is the difference between a shippable container and a Customs hold.
  • Factory identity: registration number, city, and whether they name audited partner factories. A real buying office states a company, a location and a contact you can verify — not just a chat widget.
  • MOQ and consolidation minimum: per-SKU MOQ and the container minimum. For a program mixing five to ten factories into one FCL, the consolidation minimum matters more than any single-SKU MOQ.
  • Inspection standard: an explicit AQL level (2.5 is common for general retail) with a downloadable report, not "we check everything."
  • Private-label capability: UPC/EAN barcodes, retail-ready color boxes, gift-set assembly. Search "private label" and "UPC" on their domain to confirm they handle retail packaging, not just bulk.

Reading the results like a buyer

Operator results still need judgment. Three rules keep you honest:

  • Dated evidence beats vague claims. A certificate PDF with an issue date and an expiry you can check is worth more than a homepage line that says "fully certified." Note the dates — an expired LFGB file is worse than none, because it looks like a pass.
  • Specific numbers beat superlatives. "3–5% commission," "AQL 2.5," "~US$20,000 per container" are hard to fake and easy to verify. "Competitive prices" and "top quality" cost nothing to write.
  • A document you can open beats a badge you can't. If a site shows a compliance logo but your filetype:pdf search finds no matching certificate, treat the badge as marketing until proven otherwise.

This is exactly the discipline a dollar-store or supermarket buyer needs: the homepage wants you to feel safe, the operator query shows you whether you are. The two rarely match, and the gap is where your risk lives.

From mined facts to a verified shortlist

Search operators get you candidates with their own claims attached; they do not get you a partner. Treat what you find as a pre-screen, then send each surviving domain the same RFQ you would send anyone: shelf categories, per-SKU quantities, inspection standard, food-contact requirements, destination port, and container minimum. Ask them to confirm the facts you already pulled from their own site. The ones that reply matching the documents you found — dated certificates, the same MOQ, the same commission line — were probably the right matches, and you surfaced them in minutes instead of after three email rounds. The ones that dodge or contradict their own pages were weak matches all along; the query just exposed them before you wired a deposit.

You do not need to become an SEO expert to do this. You need five operators, a one-page comparison, and the habit of trusting a document over a slogan. The buying offices that treat their own facts as commitments are easy to find once you stop reading the homepage and start searching the domain.
