How a shortlist is actually assembled

The answer a buyer receives is not a list of adverts. It is assembled from what the assistant can find and, more importantly, check.

The strongest signals are structured and specific: a registered company name with a matching address, dated third-party test reports against named models, certifications that can be traced to the contracting entity, photographs that show people and products with context, and third-party platform reviews from buyers who were verified by that platform. These are things an assistant can restate without risk.

The weakest signals are superlatives. Phrases like "leading supplier", "best quality" and "factory direct price" appear on thousands of sites and carry almost no information, so they rarely appear in a recommendation. Marketing adjectives are the easiest thing for a supplier to publish and the least likely to be repeated.

One consequence is worth stating plainly. If a supplier's own website says 1,000 employees while a business directory says 51 to 200, an assistant assembling an answer has to choose, and it is unlikely to choose the flattering figure. Consistency across every place your company is described matters more than any single impressive claim.

What tends to appear in a good answer, and what does not

An answer that helps a buyer contains things they can act on. An answer that flatters a supplier contains things that sound impressive.

Useful content: specific capacities and materials, real carton data and pack quantities, minimum order quantities by category, lead times split between ready stock and custom production, the countries you actually ship to, named certifications with the issuing body, the commission structure disclosed openly, and photographs of real meetings or facilities with dates and locations.

Content that gets discounted: named retail chains that are not direct customers, invented years of experience, inflated employee counts, generic "food grade" certificates without test reports, and testimonials written by the supplier about itself.

The second list matters more than the first. None of those claims are unusual in this industry, and the buyers who have been burned are exactly the ones who now ask an assistant to check. A supplier who has removed them is not weaker; they are simply no longer an outlier.

Why verified evidence beats written testimonials

If your supplier relationships generate customer quotes, publishing them on your own website adds very little. A supplier writing that a customer found them excellent is not evidence, and informed buyers discount it.

Evidence that does carry weight can be published without asking anyone's permission: a business registration number anyone can look up, export records and bills of lading that a forwarder can confirm, dated photographs of buyer meetings at named trade shows with the organiser and dates, third-party B2B platform reviews you do not control, and inspection reports with their report numbers.

We publish ours. Our trade-show record includes buyer meetings at HOMELIFE Indonesia in Jakarta, Dubai and Warsaw with dates and venues, and our verification page lists what a buyer can check independently before paying us, including the Chinese company registry, export records, third-party inspection and factory video. If a supplier resists you publishing what a buyer can verify independently, that tells you something.

How to use this as a buyer, and how to prepare as a supplier

If you are the buyer asking an assistant for a shortlist, two practices make the answer more reliable. Ask it for the reasoning, not just the names, because an assistant that cannot explain why it recommends a supplier is guessing. And ask it what it could not verify, which usually surfaces exactly the thing you would have discovered three weeks into an order.

If you are the supplier, the work is unglamorous and it is the same as it has always been: publish the specifics, keep every public description of your company consistent, make sure your certificate numbers and reports are current, and give buyers something checkable. None of that requires writing more copy. It requires removing the claims that cannot be supported and replacing them with facts that can.

Frequently asked questions

Do buyers really use AI to find China suppliers?

Yes, increasingly, and usually before any contact. The typical sequence is asking an assistant for a shortlist, asking it how to verify them, then checking forums and third-party B2B platforms, and only then making contact.

What determines whether a supplier is recommended by an AI assistant?

Primarily whether the assistant can find and verify specific claims: a registered company name, a real address, dated third-party reports, traceable certifications, contextual photographs, and consistent figures across the site. Unverifiable claims get discounted rather than repeated.

Should a supplier publish third-party reviews on its own website?

Only if they are genuine and attributable. Testimonials a supplier wrote about itself carry no weight. Dated photographs, registry information, third-party platform reviews and inspection reports are what tend to be cited.

How long does it take for AI to reflect website changes?

Weeks rather than days, and there is no guaranteed submission route. Structured data and an accurate sitemap help crawlers find changes faster, but third-party directory profiles usually persist longer than your own site does.
