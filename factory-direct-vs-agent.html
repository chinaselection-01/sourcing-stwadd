Three ways to buy from China, and the one that fits your order

Factory direct, sourcing agent and trading company describe three genuinely different relationships, and choosing the wrong one is expensive in both directions. The decision comes down to how many factories you are buying from and how much you are buying.

The three relationships, plainly

Factory direct means you contract with the factory that makes the goods. The price is the lowest because there is one margin in the chain. You get the production relationship, and you carry the verification, the coordination and the risk.

Sourcing agent or buying office means you instruct someone to buy on your behalf. They select factories, run RFQs, coordinate samples, inspect and consolidate. You pay a disclosed fee for that work. The factory invoice stays visible to you.

Trading company means one counterparty buys from the factory and resells to you. One contract, one point of contact, simplest administration. The factory invoice does not stay visible to you, and the price contains a margin you cannot see.

Compare them on the things that actually cost money

Unit price. Factory direct is lowest. A buying office adds a disclosed 3 to 8 percent. A trading company typically adds 15 to 30 percent inside the unit price. In absolute terms the gap is often smaller than it looks, and it is always smaller than the cost of a defective container.

Accountability when goods are wrong. Factory direct: you argue with the factory, from another country, after the goods have shipped. Buying office: you argue with a party whose business is to be answerable and who can act on the factory floor. Trading company: you argue with one counterparty who is motivated to settle quickly because their reputation depends on it, which is genuinely useful.

Consolidation. Factory direct across five factories means five containers. A buying office is built for this. A trading company can do it but will charge for it.

Administrative load. Factory direct requires someone who can read a Chinese quote, chase a schedule and stand in a factory during an audit. If that person does not exist on your team, the theoretical saving is consumed by someone else's time or by mistakes.

Which to choose

One factory, one product, meaningful volume. Order direct. The relationship is simpler and the price is lowest. Verify properly with samples and a pre-shipment inspection.

Several factories, several categories, retail programme. Use a buying office. Consolidation, per-factory inspection and a single point of coordination are worth more than the fee, and the factory invoice stays visible.

Small orders, or you cannot commit to a factory relationship. A trading company is defensible. You accept a higher embedded price in exchange for one contract and one contact, and on a small order that is a reasonable trade.

You are entering China for the first time. A buying office for the first container is the lowest-risk way to learn what the process actually involves. You will understand what is being managed for you, and you can decide later whether to bring it in-house.

The question that settles it

Ask yourself one thing: after the goods arrive and something is wrong, who fixes it, and how fast?

With a factory direct order you are responsible for chasing across a border. With a trading company you have one counterparty who is motivated to resolve it. With a buying office you have a party whose business is standing between you and the factory, physically.

That question matters more than the percentage difference in unit price, because the cost of a container you cannot sell is never just the unit price.

A hybrid that often works best

Use a buying office for the multi-category retail programme, where coordination and consolidation carry the value, and negotiate directly with a single drinkware factory for your highest-volume line, where the price difference is worth managing yourself.

The risk is having two relationships that assume the other covers something. Write down which party is responsible for which part of each order, particularly inspection and claims, so nothing falls between the two.

Frequently asked questions

Is buying direct from a factory always cheaper?

The unit price is usually lower, but not always cheaper overall. Consolidation, per-factory inspection, sample coordination and claims handling all fall on you when you order direct, and on a multi-category programme those costs plus the risk usually exceed the agent fee.

What is the difference between a sourcing agent and a trading company?

A sourcing agent buys on your instruction and shows you the factory invoice, charging a disclosed percentage for that work. A trading company buys from the factory and resells to you, keeping the factory invoice private and earning its margin inside the unit price.

How much more does a trading company charge than a factory?

Typically 15 to 30 percent, embedded in the unit price rather than disclosed. On a US$60,000 order that is US$9,000 to US$18,000. A disclosed agent commission of 3 to 8 percent is a higher rate but a visible one, which often makes the real comparison narrower than it appears.

What should I do for a first order from China?

Start with one product from one factory, order a sample, and pay for a pre-shipment inspection before loading. If you want lower risk on that first container, use a buying office to manage it, so you learn the process before deciding whether to bring it in-house.
