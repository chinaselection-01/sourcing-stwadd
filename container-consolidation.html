Five factories should not mean five containers

A retail chain buying across six categories from six factories will, by default, receive six containers, six sets of freight invoices and six customs clearances. Container consolidation puts those goods into one container with one bill of lading, and the saving is usually larger than most buyers expect.

How consolidation works

The goods arrive at a consolidation warehouse, usually in your city of consolidation, from each factory separately. Each supplier's cartons are counted, photographed and labelled against that supplier's packing list. When the total reaches a full container load, everything is loaded together, one export declaration is filed, and one bill of lading is issued against the container.

On arrival, the consignee receives the full container and the individual packing lists, and distributes the cartons to their own distribution centres. What you lose is the ability to return a single supplier's goods without opening and sorting the whole container, which is why the contractual and labelling discipline matters more than usual.

What it actually saves

Ocean freight from Ningbo or Shanghai to Rotterdam or Los Angeles is charged per container, not per kilo. Splitting a retail program across four FCLs means paying four ocean freights, four origin handling charges, four terminal fees and four customs entries, for a fraction of a container each.

For a program that fills half a container across four suppliers, consolidation typically removes most of the fixed cost structure from three shipments and replaces it with one. Beyond the freight, a single container means one delivery window to manage rather than four, and one arrival to clear rather than four.

The counter-case matters too. If your goods already fill a container from one factory, consolidation adds cost and handling for nothing. And when every item is fragile, consolidation increases the risk of transit damage because mixed cartons shift differently than uniform pallets.

The three risks that turn consolidation into a problem

Inaccurate or aggregated customs declaration. Customs requires each item to be declared accurately. Consolidating under a generic description is how containers get held for inspection, and how back duty and penalties occur. Declare each product properly even when it shares a container, and keep per-supplier packing lists and photographs.

Unclear liability when something arrives damaged. Damage discovered on arrival cannot be attributed to a specific factory if the cartons were mixed at the warehouse. Use a carton label that identifies the factory and a photograph taken at consolidation, and state the damage-allocation rule in your contract before the first shipment.

Supplier quality slipping out of sight. A factory with a 2 percent defect rate is tolerable. Five factories with a 2 percent defect rate produce a container with a 2 percent defect rate and no obvious source. That is why inspection happens before consolidation, at each factory, not after loading.

How to set up a consolidated shipment properly

Agree the format before the first order. The specification sheet should state: who inspects and when, who labels cartons and how, how photographs are taken and shared, what the packing list per supplier must contain, and how damage claims are allocated.

Build a delivery schedule backwards from your warehouse, allowing two to four weeks of ocean transit plus a buffer for customs. Late consolidation is common in peak season because factories run late and the booking deadline passes while you are still waiting for the last supplier.

Keep one contact responsible for the whole container. The most common failure in consolidation is not a factory problem but a coordination problem: nobody owns the complete list.

When consolidation is not worth it

Three situations. Your volume already fills a container from a single factory. Every item is fragile enough that mixed loading is a bigger risk than the freight saving. Or the consolidation agent charges a per-crate handling fee that exceeds the ocean freight you saved. Always ask for the delivered cost comparison rather than the freight rate alone, because the handling fee is where the margin hides.

Frequently asked questions

What is container consolidation in China sourcing?

It is combining goods from several factories or suppliers into one container so you pay one ocean freight, one bill of lading and one customs entry instead of several. Each supplier's goods are received at a consolidation warehouse, counted, labelled and loaded together.

How much does consolidation save?

Ocean freight is charged per container, so splitting a program across four half-full containers means paying the fixed cost four times. Savings vary widely by program, and handling fees at the warehouse can eat into the difference, so ask for a delivered cost comparison rather than a freight rate.

What are the main risks of consolidated shipments?

Inaccurate customs declaration when items are aggregated under one description, unclear liability when damaged goods cannot be attributed to a specific factory, and quality issues slipping through because each factory was inspected in isolation rather than as part of the finished container.

Can I consolidate a container with both drinkware and other goods?

Yes, that is the usual case, provided each item is declared accurately and packaging prevents movement in transit. Avoid mixing liquids or fragile glassware with heavy items, and keep per-supplier packing lists and photographs so any claim can be attributed correctly.
