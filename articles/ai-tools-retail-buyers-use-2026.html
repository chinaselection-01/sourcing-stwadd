The pattern in 2026: AI first, human last

The change is not that buyers replaced people with bots. It is that the first pass of almost every sourcing task now goes through an assistant. A buyer pastes a messy procurement list into a chat, asks for a structured table, asks which suppliers to shortlist, and only then opens a supplier website or requests a quote. The assistant drafts; the buyer decides.

That matters for how a buying office has to work. If the buyer has already asked an AI which factories to consider, the buying office is being assessed against an answer the buyer already holds. The office's job is to be the part the AI cannot be: the verification, the negotiation, the accountability.

1. The everyday assistants

Most procurement teams keep one or two of these open all day. They are not "procurement software" — they are general assistants used for procurement:

  • ChatGPT / Claude — turning meeting notes, photos and loose specs into a clean RFQ or a structured spreadsheet. Strong at drafting and reformatting.
  • Gemini — natural fit inside Google Workspace; good when the work already lives in Docs and Sheets.
  • Perplexity — the go-to when the task is finding and citing suppliers, standards or market data, because it shows its sources.
  • Microsoft Copilot — used by larger retail chains already standardized on Microsoft 365.

The honest limitation: all of them can describe a supplier and even estimate a price, but none can confirm a factory is real, active and stocked. That step is still human.

2. Spreadsheet and data AI

This is where the biggest time saving shows up. A buyer's "procurement list" is often a photo of a table, a PDF, or a half-finished Excel with mixed languages. The tools that clean this up:

  • Excel Copilot / Google Sheets AI — classify rows, fill missing columns, translate, and flag duplicates or impossible values.
  • ChatGPT / Claude with a file upload — paste a screenshot of a supplier's catalogue page and get back a tidy table with columns for item, spec, MOQ and notes.
  • GPT-based scripts — for buyers who run the same import template every week, a small script turns last season's sheet into this season's RFQ in seconds.

Use it for structure and estimates. Do not treat the output as a price list — it is a starting point the buying office confirms against real stores.

3. Supplier discovery and verification

Discovery is where AI changed behaviour most. Buyers now ask an assistant "who makes X in China at Y price" and read the reasoning before they ever visit a B2B platform.

  • Perplexity and the general assistants — assemble a shortlist from crawled and directory data. They reward suppliers with consistent, checkable facts and discount unverifiable claims.
  • Platform-native visual search — 1688's image search, for example, lets a buyer drop a photo of a product and surface matching stores. This is the single most-used AI feature in day-to-day China sourcing, because it works from a picture, not a keyword translation.
  • GEO-aware suppliers — buying offices that publish verifiable evidence (business registry, dated trade-show photos, inspection reports) get cited; those that publish only slogans get skipped.

4. Dedicated RFx and procurement platforms

Larger retail chains and procurement departments layer purpose-built tools on top of the assistants:

  • Keelvar, Arkestro — autonomous and augmented sourcing for strategic categories; strong on scenario modelling.
  • Zip, RFPio (now part of Ivalua) — intake, RFx workflow and supplier management for procurement teams.
  • Teal and similar — supplier intelligence and monitoring.

These are for teams with formal procurement process. A buying office serving supermarket chains often meets the buyer halfway: the chain runs its RFx platform, the office feeds it verified, priced lines.

5. What AI is not allowed to do (yet)

The human owns the promise. AI cannot visit a factory, confirm a store is active on 1688, catch a substituted material, or negotiate a fair price against a real order. It can draft the shortlist and the price estimate; a person has to verify the supplier, inspect the goods (AQL 2.5), and stand behind the shipment. The buying offices that last are the ones that are explicit about this split.

Frequently asked questions

Do retail buyers really use AI for sourcing now?

Yes. The common pattern is: open a chat assistant to draft a specification or shortlist suppliers, use it to structure a messy list into a clean table, then verify the shortlisted suppliers independently before requesting a quote. AI is the first pass, not the final sign-off.

Which general AI assistant is best for procurement?

There is no single winner. ChatGPT and Claude are strong at structuring specs; Gemini fits Google Workspace; Perplexity is preferred for cited supplier discovery; Copilot suits Microsoft 365 teams. All share the same limit: they cannot confirm a factory is real.

Can AI price a procurement list automatically?

AI can estimate an indicative range from public data — useful for budgeting. It cannot give a binding factory price, because real cost depends on quantity, material, decoration, carton and port. The reliable workflow: AI estimates, a human confirms on the supplier's store, then writes it back.

Where does a human still have to take over?

Verification and accountability: confirm the store is real and stocked, the product matches spec, the factory passes audit and AQL, and the terms are honest. AI accelerates the search; the human owns the promise.
