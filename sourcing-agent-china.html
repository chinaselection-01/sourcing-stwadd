A sourcing agent is a purchasing function, not a middleman

Most people looking for a sourcing agent in China want one thing: to buy from Chinese factories without taking on the errors that come with distance. What actually makes an agent valuable is not access to factories, which anyone can claim, but accountability for what those factories deliver.

What a sourcing agent does for you

Four jobs, in the order they matter.

Supplier screening. Shortlisting factories that genuinely run your process, then filtering out those that will quote anything. A useful shortlist is three to five names per category, each with a reason attached: what line they run, what their minimum is, what they are weak at.

Quote comparison. Putting every factory on the same specification and comparing the results as landed cost, not as unit price. A quote that omits packaging, inspection or freight is not comparable and the gap is usually hiding in the omitted line.

Sample and production follow-up. Ordering samples, consolidating your feedback, chasing a production schedule, and holding the factory to an approved pre-production sample before the run.

Inspection. Being on site at the factory when the goods are packed, with the authority to stop a shipment that does not meet the specification. This is the part that distinguishes an agent from a message-forwarder.

What it actually costs

Three structures are common, and two of them hide something.

Percentage of order value, 3 to 8 percent. Transparent and easy to budget. This is the structure we use, at 3 to 5 percent.

Percentage of the factory price. Similar, but the base is sometimes padded, so ask what the base actually is.

Fixed fee per project. Predictable, and fair for larger programs, but an agent working on a fixed fee has no incentive to spend time on your problem.

What to watch for is a quoted rate that is low and then increases at the confirmation stage, or a fee described vaguely as handling. Get the number in writing before you send specifications.

Three warning signs that should end the conversation

They will not let you speak to a factory. Every legitimate agent allows it. An agent who refuses has a reason, and it is not your benefit. Ask for a video call with the factory you chose, on your schedule.

They cannot show you the factory invoice. If the fee is a percentage on top of factory cost, the invoice exists. An agent who says it is confidential is describing a margin, not a fee.

They dismiss third-party inspection. Any supplier confident in its own quality has no reason to object to an SGS or BV inspection. Objecting usually means the goods are not finished, or production was subcontracted somewhere you have not seen.

What to have ready before you brief an agent

An agent can only work from a specification. The more precise your brief, the shorter the process and the more comparable the quotes.

Specify: category, capacity or dimensions, material and grade, lid or closure type, decoration method, packaging format, target landed price, destination port and expected quantity. If you do not know the material grade, ask for 304 rather than accepting stainless steel, since 201 looks identical and costs less.

Also decide what happens on failure before it happens: your defect priorities, the AQL level you want, and whether you would rather have the goods sorted and reworked than refunded. Agreeing that in advance makes the failure conversation much shorter.

Choosing between a buying office and a broker

A China buying office sits inside the supply chain: it selects suppliers, consolidates, inspects and manages production. A broker arranges freight and customs. They are usually not alternatives, and buying the same service twice is a common way to overpay.

If your problem is goods quality, you need a buying office. If your problem is getting a container through customs, you need a broker. Many retail chains need the first and use the second only at the end.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a sourcing agent in China cost?

Typically 3 to 8 percent of order value, or a fixed project fee for larger programs. We charge a flat 3 to 5 percent against the factory invoice with inspection and consolidation included. Be cautious of rates well below that range, because the cost usually reappears as hidden markup in the unit price.

What should I ask before hiring a sourcing agent?

Ask whether you can speak directly to a factory, whether they will show you the factory invoice behind a quote, how they handle goods that fail inspection, and who pays when goods do not match. The quality of these answers tells you more than any portfolio they show you.

Is it cheaper to go direct to a factory?

For a single product in volume from one factory, yes, ordering direct is simpler and cheaper. For multi-category retail programs where each factory has a small quantity, going direct means multiple containers, multiple shipments and no consolidated inspection, which usually costs more than the agent fee.

Can a sourcing agent guarantee quality?

No responsible agent will. Inspection is a statistical sample, so defects can pass. What you can hold an agent to is process: a defined AQL level, an agreed defect classification, inspection before loading, a written report, and a contractual remedy when goods fail.
